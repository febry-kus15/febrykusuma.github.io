Building an industrial zone on a floodplain

A technical feasibility assessment for a 177-hectare Green Special Economic Zone in Kandal Province — where the hardest design question was not what to build, but how to keep it dry.

The brief

Cambodia is preparing to graduate from Least Developed Country status by 2029. To get there, it needs to move beyond garments — which still make up the majority of its exports — into higher-value manufacturing. A Green Special Economic Zone was proposed as the vehicle for that shift.

The study was commissioned under the UK Government's Green Cities, Infrastructure and Energy Programme, funded by the FCDO, and formed the technical sub-package of a wider seven-part feasibility study run with the Royal Government of Cambodia. Our scope was deliberately bounded: establish whether the site could physically be developed, and at what cost. Market demand, investment structuring and the environmental and social impact assessment sat with other teams.

The site sits in Kien Svay District, Kandal Province — roughly 35 km southeast of Phnom Penh, 3.5 km from the PPAP container terminal at LM17, and 25 km from the new Techo International Airport. On paper, the logistics are excellent.

The problem nobody could design around

The site sits inside the Mekong floodplain, on flat ground at roughly +2.5 to +3.5 m above sea level, in what is effectively a shallow basin. It floods. Not occasionally — annually, through the June-to-October monsoon, with waters typically receding around November or December.

When the LiDAR topographic survey was flown in January 2026, approximately 92% of the site was still covered by surface water. The survey team had to classify the site into land, shallow water, boat tracks and depressions, then apply measured water depths to estimate ground levels underneath.

Fathom global flood modelling indicated inundation up to 6.18 m under a 1-in-1,000-year event — an elevation of 9.18 m against existing ground of around 3.0 m.

Flooding here is not purely natural. Local authorities open sluice gates to deliberately flood the basin in support of rice farming, and additional water arrives via Mekong seepage and discharge from Boeng Thum lake to the north. Nobody could tell us the frequency or duration of those gate openings, and no regional hydrological model was available to us. That uncertainty is flagged throughout the report as something capable of invalidating key findings.

Two ways to stay dry

Rather than prescribe a single answer, we developed two masterplan options distinguished almost entirely by their approach to flood mitigation. Both draw on local precedent: the full-fill approach mirrors nearby Suvannaphum SEZ, while the dyke approach follows Techo International Airport.

Option 1

Complete fill

Raise the entire site above the 1-in-1,000-year flood level, to +10.18 m with 1 m freeboard.

  • Average fill height 7.18 m
  • 12.68 million m³ of imported fill
  • Single central retention pond, full gravity drainage
  • Operates through extreme floods with no mechanical dependency
CAPEX USD 324.6m · USD 183/m²
Option 2

Dyke with partial fill

Ring the site with a dyke to +10.18 m, raise the platform only to the 1-in-100-year level, +7.55 m.

  • Average fill height 4.55 m
  • 8.25 million m³ of imported fill — two thirds of Option 1
  • Perimeter conveyance canal, pump-assisted discharge
  • Lower cost, but ongoing pump maintenance and less usable land
CAPEX USD 305.6m · USD 172/m²

To put the fill volumes in perspective: Option 1 would need roughly 507,200 truck loads at 25 m³ each. Running 100 trucks ten hours a day, with a source within 5 km and year-round access, that is about 1.4 years of continuous hauling — and no fill source has been identified.

Critically, the two options are functionally equivalent above ground. Same spatial structure, same 70% development efficiency, same plot strategy, same circulation framework, same sustainability targets. The choice between them is not an urban design decision — it is a question of earthworks risk, constructability, phasing and long-term maintenance.

The masterplan

Both options organise around a primary spine road that carries movement, infrastructure and identity. The largest industrial plots sit centrally along that spine for anchor tenants; ready-built factories cluster to the south, where the site's non-rectangular geometry suits finer-grain subdivision.

All industrial plots carry a 60% building coverage ratio, 1.0 floor area ratio and a mandated 10% green space per parcel — which lifts the site-wide green and blue provision from 15.8% of public open space to around 22.8% in total.

Designing for the people who work there

An estate of this scale carries roughly 30,000 workers. On-site dormitories accommodate about 9,000 — some 30% of the workforce — reducing commuting demand and associated emissions.

Social infrastructure is organised into three clusters: food court and retail; healthcare and emergency, including a 24-hour ambulance base and fire station; and an integrated amenity centre. That last one matters. It includes a childcare centre, lactation rooms for nursing mothers, women-only breakrooms, a women's crisis management centre, training facilities and remittance services — provisions that reflect the fact that Cambodia's manufacturing workforce is predominantly female.

Moving 30,000 people

There is no formal public transport near the site today — movement is by motorbike, tuk-tuk and small truck. The transport strategy therefore builds a self-contained system: two internal e-shuttle loops and one external route, sized to carry around 4,000 employees during peak hour with a fleet of 15–18 electric shuttles, four 150 kW DC chargers, nine transit shelters and a terminal depot.

Around 2,400 parking spaces are provided across the estate, of which roughly 10% are EV-ready or fitted with chargers, spanning 7 kW for e-motorcycles up to 300 kW for e-trucks and buses. A further 10% are wheelchair accessible.

A four-lane access road connecting to the Ring Road 3 extension is required — the site currently has none. A multi-criteria assessment of three alignments favoured the eastern RR3 connection: the shortest new build at 4.3 km, no houses affected, minimal environmental impact and the lowest cost. Peak-hour modelling puts the road network at Level of Service A internally and B at the main gate.

Where it sits

The site occupies a flat basin between the Bassac and Mekong rivers, with the future Funan Techo Canal planned nearby — an alignment that could materially change the site's hydrology, and about which no information was available to the team.

Infrastructure from nothing

The site has no utilities whatsoever. Everything connects from outside, and several systems had to be designed as closed loops to reduce that external dependency.

Water recycling does the heaviest sustainability lifting. A centralised plant prioritising grey and black water reuse delivers around 4,863 m³/day — roughly 65% of total estate demand — which comfortably exceeds the brief's 20% reduction target and substantially cuts reliance on external supply.

The green targets, honestly assessed

Of 25 green design elements in the brief, 14 are achievable within this scope. The remaining 11 sit beyond a feasibility study — energy efficiency measures, GHG accounting per capita, construction waste diversion — and need detailed design and operational data.

Renewable energy is where the plan falls short of ambition. Rooftop solar across all industrial lots could deliver around 100 GWh annually, about 30% of the estate's 329 GWh demand. Add waste-to-energy and you reach roughly 55%. The brief asked for 60% at initial phase and over 80% at build-out. We said so plainly, and noted that Renewable Energy Certificates offer a credible route to close the gap.

On certification, both options could achieve BREEAM ratings of Excellent to Outstanding. Within the credits assessable at this stage, the plan captures 69% for BREEAM Communities and 79% for BREEAM Infrastructure.

What it costs, and why that number is large

Regional benchmarks for Southeast Asian industrial parks without major flood mitigation run around USD 120–150/m². This site comes in at USD 172–183/m². The difference is almost entirely flood risk — earthworks and site formation alone account for 31–38% of total CAPEX.

A 2013 JICA study for a nearby 145 ha SEZ estimated USD 60 million. Our figure is roughly five times that, and we spent a full section of the report explaining why the two are not comparable: thirteen years of cost inflation, a 23% larger site, higher platform levels, no reuse of on-site fill material, ground improvement that JICA did not consider, and more than three times the power provision.

Flood risk is the dominant technical constraint and the primary driver of cost variability — potentially more than 35% at concept design stage.

What I took from it

01

Say what you don't know

The report opens with "Key Known Unknowns" — hydrology, the Funan Canal, fill sourcing, the ESIA. Naming them up front made the findings more useful, not less credible.

02

Options, not answers

Presenting two technically viable options without a recommendation kept the decision with the people who hold the wider commercial, environmental and policy picture.

03

Site conditions set the cost

The urban design barely varies between options. The ground beneath it drives a 35%+ swing in capital cost. Site analysis is not a preliminary — it is the project.
